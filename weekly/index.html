The facts

  • On November 4, CMS issued the CY 2027 Home Health Prospective Payment System final rule, CMS-1844-F. It went on public inspection that day and is scheduled for Federal Register publication November 9. This same-day action occurred before the prior archive cutoff and is recorded as a disclosed backfill discovered and inspected in this run. [1] [2]
  • CMS estimates aggregate Medicare payments to home-health agencies will rise 2.4%, or $415 million, compared with 2026. That estimate combines a 2.1% payment update and a projected 0.3% fixed-dollar-loss effect while the rule separately applies a temporary 3.0% reduction to the 2027 base rate tied to earlier Patient-Driven Groupings Model behavior assumptions. [1] [2] [3]
  • The rule makes all Medicare enrollment revocations retroactive to the date noncompliance began and adds or expands grounds for enrollment denial or revocation. It also extends some license and program-suspension consequences to owners and managing employees and allows action against certain hospices, home-health agencies and DMEPOS suppliers that fail reenrollment and survey or accreditation requirements after specified ownership changes. [1] [2] [3]
  • CMS projects the broader enrollment provisions will save about $82 million annually. That figure is an agency estimate; no post-implementation recovery, fraud reduction, beneficiary-access or due-process outcome has yet been measured. [1] [2] [3]
  • CMS also clarified that skilled palliative care can be covered under the existing home-health benefit when eligibility rules are met, while stressing that the rule creates no new benefit. It shortened an OASIS quality-data submission deadline from 135 to 45 days, which CMS says can reduce public-reporting lag by up to three months; CMS reports that about 98% of agencies already submit within 45 days. [1] [2]

Significance

The rule affects national home-health reimbursement and gives CMS broader, partly retroactive enrollment-enforcement tools across Medicare provider types. The net payment projection does not show how individual agencies fare, and the enforcement savings and access consequences remain unmeasured.

Goalpost / response

CMS says retroactive revocations and expanded denial grounds will recover improper payments, deter fraud and protect beneficiaries while preserving access through case-specific review. The tests are actual recoveries, error and appeal rates, provider exits, beneficiary access and quality-reporting timeliness—not the $82 million projection alone.

Maybe / Therefore

Maybe targeted enforcement removes high-risk providers without disrupting care, or retroactivity produces disputes and access losses in concentrated markets; individual agencies may also experience results unlike the aggregate 2.4% estimate. Therefore the archive records finalized payment and enrollment policy, not proven savings, fraud reduction or uniform provider gains.

This same-day action occurred before the prior archive cutoff and is recorded as a disclosed backfill discovered and inspected in this run. [1] [2]
  • For hospitals and ASCs meeting applicable quality-reporting requirements, CMS finalized 2.3% payment-rate updates, based on a projected 3.2% market-basket increase reduced by a 0.9 percentage-point productivity adjustment. [1] [2] [3]
  • CMS extended site-neutral payment to imaging without contrast in excepted off-campus hospital departments, using Physician Fee Schedule-equivalent rates and exempting rural sole community hospitals. CMS projects about $260 million in lower 2027 Part B spending, including about $70 million each in lower beneficiary premiums and lower cost-sharing; these are estimates, not measured savings. [1] [2] [3]
  • For separately payable drugs acquired through the 340B program, CMS finalized payment at average sales price minus 28%. It projects $4.35 billion less in Original Medicare drug payments and $1.1 billion less in beneficiary drug payments in the first year, with an average $900 saving for a qualifying beneficiary. Statute requires budget neutrality, so CMS says non-drug OPPS payments will rise by an equivalent amount. [1] [2] [3]
  • The rule also accelerates the annual offset used to recoup the earlier 340B remedy from 0.5% to 2%, removes 637 additional services from the inpatient-only list, expands selected prior authorization and provider-identifier requirements and gives safety measures greater weight in hospital star ratings. The AHA objected that the hospital update is inadequate and said the site-neutral and 340B changes may strain rural and underserved access; those are attributed stakeholder warnings, not measured outcomes. [1] [2]

Significance

This rule reallocates billions of projected Medicare payments among drugs and other outpatient services and expands site-neutral payment. It can lower some beneficiary liabilities while reducing particular hospital revenue streams, making distributional effects and access consequences as important as the headline aggregate projections.

Goalpost / response

CMS says acquisition-cost evidence justifies lower 340B drug reimbursement and that site-neutral payment prevents beneficiaries from paying more solely because of setting. Hospitals argue that outpatient departments treat more complex patients and that the reductions threaten access. The tests are actual claims, beneficiary costs, hospital service availability and rural or safety-net access after implementation.

Maybe / Therefore

Maybe budget-neutral non-drug increases, statutory exemptions and service migration offset some hospital losses, or litigation and later rulemaking alter implementation. Therefore the archive records finalized payment formulas and agency projections, not guaranteed beneficiary savings or a proven access loss.
